# U.S. Real Estate JV Setup Project Plan

Date: 2026-06-03

Purpose: Turn the structuring memo into a practical startup roadmap for a four-partner Midwest real estate flipping venture with two U.S. citizens, one U.K. investor, and one Belgian investor.

Important limitation: This is a business implementation guide, not a legal opinion or tax opinion. The structure should be reviewed by U.S. tax counsel, a corporate attorney, a CPA experienced with foreign-owned U.S. corporations, and U.K./Belgian advisors before money moves.

## Updated Partner Facts

| Fact | Updated Answer |
|---|---|
| Risk appetite | No high-risk strategies or workarounds |
| Foreign investor tax residence | Both the U.K. national and Belgian national are UAE tax residents |
| Treaty posture | Do not assume U.K. or Belgium treaty benefits; no U.S.-UAE income tax treaty baseline |
| Foreign investor activity | Passive capital only; no U.S.-based services |
| First operating state | Wisconsin |
| Phase 1, Step 2 | Broken out into a separate decision tree: foreign-investor-decision-tree.md/html/docx |

These facts change the planning priority. The conservative base case should not rely on aggressive portfolio-interest planning or treaty-country workarounds. The planning focus should move toward clean ownership, estate-tax protection, bankability, Wisconsin compliance, and proper withholding.

## Executive Summary

The most practical baseline is:

1. Form one U.S. C corporation as the tax blocker and operating parent.
2. Use separate disregarded LLCs under that corporation for property title, liability separation, and state operations where useful.
3. Do not let the foreign partners directly own the U.S. corporation until estate tax planning is reviewed.
4. Treat foreign money as either equity or properly documented debt. Do not assume shareholder debt qualifies for the portfolio interest exemption if a foreign lender owns 10% or more of the corporation's voting power.
5. Open U.S. banking only after the entity structure, EIN, ownership chart, capital plan, and source-of-funds package are ready.
6. Build withholding, Form 5472, Form 1042/1042-S, and state compliance into the operating calendar from day one.

Plain-English bottom line: the U.S. blocker helps keep the foreign partners from being directly in the U.S. real estate business, but it does not make the venture tax-free. The best financial setup is the one that balances tax efficiency with defensibility. The highest-risk shortcuts are weak shareholder loans, undocumented related-party interest, direct foreign ownership of U.S. shares without estate planning, and casual money movement through personal accounts.

## Phase 1 - Decisions Before Formation

Do this before filing any entity paperwork. These decisions affect taxes, banking, partner rights, and exit planning.

| Step | What To Do | Why It Matters | Professional Review |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Confirm tax residence | Confirm whether the U.K. and Belgian partners are tax residents of the U.K., Belgium, UAE, or somewhere else. | Treaty benefits depend on tax residence and beneficial ownership, not nationality or where the money sits. | U.S. international tax counsel plus U.K./Belgian advisors |
| 2. Confirm investor type | Decide whether each foreign partner invests personally, through a foreign company, through a trust, or through a new holding company. | This drives estate tax, treaty rates, bank KYC, withholding forms, and reporting. | U.S. tax counsel and foreign counsel |
| 3. Decide ownership economics | Write down voting rights, profit split, losses, preferred return, capital calls, and exit rights. | Tax treatment follows the legal and economic deal. A vague deal creates tax and partner disputes. | Corporate attorney and CPA |
| 4. Choose first states | Pick the first Midwest states for acquisitions. | State registrations, tax returns, title rules, and local counsel needs are state-specific. | Real estate attorney and CPA |
| 5. Decide who manages | Identify who signs contracts, deals with brokers, approves purchases, and controls bank wires. | Banks, title companies, and auditors will ask who has authority. | Corporate attorney |
| 6. Decide whether foreign partners perform U.S. work | Foreign partners should avoid U.S.-based services unless planned. | U.S. services may create personal filing, visa, payroll, and withholding issues. | Immigration and tax counsel |

Deliverable: a one-page structure decision sheet signed off by the partners and advisors.

## Phase 2 - Professional Team

This structure should not be set up by a generic incorporator alone. The professional team should be small but specialized.

| Professional | What They Should Review | Why Needed |
|---|---|---|
| U.S. international tax attorney | Blocker structure, foreign ownership, treaty eligibility, withholding, estate tax, debt/equity, FIRPTA, exit planning | This is the core risk area |
| U.S. CPA | Form 1120, Form 5472, 1042/1042-S, state returns, bookkeeping design, 163(j), property accounting | Keeps the structure compliant after setup |
| Corporate attorney | C corporation charter, bylaws, shareholder agreement, note documents, board consents, transfer restrictions | Makes the business deal enforceable |
| Real estate attorney/title counsel | Purchase contracts, title LLCs, closing certificates, state transfer rules, title insurance | Prevents closing-level tax and title mistakes |
| U.K. tax advisor | U.K. investor's home-country tax, foreign tax credits, treaty residence, reporting | U.S. planning may create U.K. consequences |
| Belgian tax advisor | Belgian investor's home-country tax, foreign tax credits, treaty residence, reporting | U.S. planning may create Belgian consequences |
| Banking relationship manager | Bank KYC package, foreign investor requirements, source-of-funds documentation, wire process | Avoids delays and account freezes |

Professional review points that should not be skipped:

1. Whether foreign partners should own the U.S. corporation directly or through foreign holding companies.
2. Whether any shareholder debt can be used safely.
3. Whether the U.K. and Belgian treaty positions are actually available.
4. Whether dividend, interest, or redemption payments are the best repatriation route.
5. Whether the company could become a U.S. real property holding corporation for stock-sale exit purposes.
6. Whether state-level taxes make one state of formation or operation better than another.

## Phase 3 - Entity Structure

### Recommended Basic Chart

| Layer | Entity | Owner | Purpose |
|---|---|---|---|
| Foreign investor layer | To be determined for each foreign investor | U.K. and Belgian investors | Estate tax and treaty planning |
| U.S. blocker | U.S. C corporation | Partners or holding companies | Main taxpayer and operating parent |
| Property title layer | State LLCs disregarded to U.S. C corporation | U.S. C corporation | Liability segregation and state title holding |
| Project bank accounts | Accounts owned by U.S. C corporation or title LLCs | U.S. C corporation control | Cash control and audit trail |

### Key Formation Choices

| Decision | Plain-English Explanation | Recommended Approach |
|---|---|---|
| Corporation vs LLC taxed as corporation | A corporation is cleaner for foreign-owner blocking. An LLC can elect corporate tax treatment but may confuse banks and title companies. | Use a corporation unless counsel has a specific reason for an LLC election. |
| State of formation | Delaware is common for corporate law, but the company must register where it actually operates. | Use Delaware or a first operating state only after counsel weighs cost and state tax. |
| S corporation | S corporations cannot have nonresident alien shareholders. | Do not use an S corporation. |
| Title LLCs | LLCs can own individual properties while being ignored for federal tax if the C corp is the sole member. | Use when liability separation or state operations justify it. |
| Foreign holding companies | May reduce U.S. estate tax exposure for foreign partners. | Review before direct foreign ownership of U.S. shares. |

### Formation Steps

1. Choose the final structure after professional review.
2. Form the U.S. C corporation.
3. Draft bylaws, initial board consent, stock ledger, shareholder agreement, and transfer restrictions.
4. Issue shares only after the estate tax ownership question is resolved.
5. Apply for EIN using Form SS-4 or the IRS online EIN process.
6. If title LLCs are needed, form them as wholly owned subsidiaries of the C corporation.
7. If any LLC must elect corporate or disregarded treatment, confirm whether Form 8832 is needed.
8. Register to do business in each state where the company has offices, employees, repeated property activity, or state-law nexus.
9. Create a compliance calendar for federal, state, city, county, annual report, registered agent, and franchise tax deadlines.

## Phase 4 - Capitalization Plan

This is the most important financial design step.

### Simple Capital Options

| Method | What It Means | Pros | Risks |
|---|---|---|---|
| Common equity | Partner buys stock and shares upside/downside | Simple legally | Dividends are not deductible; foreign direct ownership may create estate tax risk |
| Preferred equity | Partner gets priority return before common owners | Can match investor economics | Still equity, not deductible interest |
| Shareholder loan | Partner lends money to company under a note | Interest may be deductible and principal can be repaid | High risk if not real debt or if withholding is mishandled |
| Third-party debt | Bank/private lender lends to company | Cleaner debt treatment | User stated no institutional bank debt, and terms may be less flexible |
| Hybrid structure | Mix of equity and debt | Can balance tax and economics | Requires careful legal and tax drafting |

### Recommended Capital Workflow

1. Build a 12-month acquisition budget.
2. Decide minimum cash reserves for taxes, repairs, insurance, title, legal, and failed deals.
3. Decide how much capital is permanent equity versus temporary deal funding.
4. Model each partner's expected contribution.
5. Model dividends, interest, and principal repayments separately.
6. Review whether any related-party debt is respected as real debt.
7. Set interest rates using market support, not partner preference.
8. Draft formal promissory notes before money moves.
9. Track each wire as either equity, loan principal, interest, expense reimbursement, or other.
10. Never move partner money through another partner's personal account unless counsel specifically approves.

### Debt Rules In Plain English

For a loan to be respected, it should look like a loan a real lender would make. That means:

1. Signed note.
2. Fixed principal amount.
3. Fixed or commercially supportable interest rate.
4. Maturity date.
5. Payment schedule.
6. Default rights.
7. Board approval.
8. Accounting records separating principal and interest.
9. No interest tied to property profits, appreciation, or flip margins unless tax counsel approves.
10. Actual payments when due, or properly documented amendments.

### Portfolio Interest Warning

Do not assume the portfolio interest exemption solves withholding. If a foreign lender owns 10% or more of the voting power of the U.S. corporation, interest paid to that lender generally does not qualify as portfolio interest. With four 25% partners, this is a major problem unless the voting and debt structure is redesigned and reviewed.

## Phase 5 - Banking Setup

Banking should be treated as a compliance project, not just opening an account.

### Bank Account Structure

| Account | Owner | Use |
|---|---|---|
| Main operating account | U.S. C corporation | General business cash, repairs, overhead, taxes |
| Acquisition account | U.S. C corporation | Funds reserved for property purchases |
| Tax reserve account | U.S. C corporation | Federal/state income tax and withholding reserves |
| Project subledger or account | U.S. C corporation or title LLC | Optional tracking by property |
| Escrow/title accounts | Title company | Closing funds only |

### What To Prepare For The Bank

Bring this package before asking the bank to open accounts:

| Document | Who Provides It | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Articles/certificate of incorporation | Corporate attorney or state filing office | Proves the corporation exists |
| EIN confirmation letter | IRS | Usually CP 575 or EIN confirmation page |
| Bylaws | Corporate attorney | Shows governance rules |
| Board resolution authorizing bank accounts | Corporate attorney | Names authorized signers |
| Ownership chart | Company | Must show U.S. and foreign beneficial owners |
| Shareholder agreement | Corporate attorney | Bank may request it for ownership and control |
| Passports/government IDs | Each beneficial owner | Foreign investors should expect extra KYC |
| Proof of address | Each beneficial owner | Utility bill, bank statement, or government document |
| Source-of-funds documents | Each investor | Bank statements, sale agreements, business income proof, audited statements if available |
| W-8BEN or W-8BEN-E | Foreign investors or foreign entities | Needed for tax status and withholding records |
| W-9 | U.S. company and U.S. investors where requested | Used to certify U.S. taxpayer status |
| Business plan | Company | Include property price range, flip strategy, states, expected volume, and no bank debt policy |
| Expected wire plan | Company | Countries, banks, approximate amounts, timing, purpose |

### Banking Instructions For The Partners

1. Pick a bank that is comfortable with foreign beneficial owners and real estate investment activity.
2. Tell the bank upfront that the company has U.K. and Belgian beneficial owners and may receive wires from Dubai/UAE or U.S. accounts.
3. Do not hide the Dubai source-of-funds path. Banks will likely ask for source and path of funds.
4. Each partner should prepare a source-of-funds memo explaining where the money came from and why it is available for investment.
5. Each foreign partner should provide passport, address proof, tax residence certificate if available, W-8 form, and bank statements showing the funding path.
6. The company should open accounts only in the company name, not in an individual's name.
7. All capital wires should reference the legal purpose: equity contribution, loan principal, or capital call.
8. Keep loan principal wires separate from interest payments.
9. Keep a monthly bank reconciliation and property-by-property cash ledger.
10. Before sending money back to foreign partners, the CPA must determine whether withholding applies and whether Form 1042-S reporting is required.

### Practical Bank Selection Criteria

| Criterion | Why It Matters |
|---|---|
| Handles foreign beneficial owners | Avoids account denial after formation |
| Real estate investor friendly | Understands frequent closings and title wires |
| Strong wire controls | Reduces fraud and partner disputes |
| Multiple approval levels | Useful for a four-partner venture |
| Good online banking exports | CPA needs clean data |
| Can issue account verification letters quickly | Title companies often need this |
| Comfortable with large cash balances | Company will be all-cash |

### Closing And FIRPTA Controls

At every property sale, the title company should be told that the seller is a domestic U.S. corporation. The closing file should include:

1. Seller's non-foreign certification or Form W-9.
2. Corporate good standing certificate if requested.
3. EIN confirmation.
4. Board or officer authority to sell.
5. Title LLC ownership confirmation if a title LLC is the named seller.
6. CPA/tax counsel review if the title company tries to impose FIRPTA withholding.

Plain-English point: FIRPTA withholding is aimed at foreign sellers of U.S. real estate. If the seller is the U.S. corporation or a disregarded LLC owned by the U.S. corporation, the buyer/title company should generally not treat the seller as foreign.

## Phase 7 - Operating Controls For Flipping Business

### Property Acquisition Checklist

1. Deal source and underwriting sheet.
2. Purchase contract reviewed by real estate attorney.
3. Title search and title insurance quote.
4. Entity buyer confirmed: C corporation or title LLC.
5. Repair budget.
6. Insurance quote before closing.
7. Board or investment committee approval.
8. Closing wire approval by at least two authorized people.
9. Property ledger opened in accounting system.
10. Resale plan and target margin documented.

### Accounting Setup

Use bookkeeping that separates:

1. Each property's purchase price.
2. Closing costs.
3. Repairs and improvements.
4. Holding costs.
5. Insurance.
6. Property taxes.
7. Utilities.
8. Sale proceeds.
9. Partner capital.
10. Loan principal.
11. Interest expense.
12. Dividends/distributions.
13. Tax reserves.

For flipping, properties are likely business inventory rather than long-term investments. That means the accounting should be built for dealer/inventory treatment unless the CPA says otherwise.

### Cash Control Rules

1. No personal accounts for company funds.
2. No undocumented partner advances.
3. No distributions without CPA review.
4. No interest payments to foreign partners without withholding review.
5. Two-person approval for outbound wires.
6. Keep title company wiring instructions verified by phone using known numbers.
7. Monthly partner report showing cash, inventory, pending deals, taxes reserved, and expected exits.

## Phase 8 - Repatriation Plan

Repatriation means getting money back to the U.K. and Belgian investors. This should be planned before the first payment is made.

| Method | Plain-English Meaning | Tax Result | Review Needed |
|---|---|---|---|
| Loan principal repayment | Company pays back borrowed money | Usually not withholding if true principal | CPA to confirm loan ledger |
| Interest | Company pays the lender for use of money | Deductible if respected; withholding may apply unless treaty/exemption applies | Tax counsel and CPA |
| Dividend | Company pays owners from profits | Not deductible; withholding generally applies unless treaty reduces it | CPA and treaty review |
| Return of capital | Company returns invested capital | Depends on basis and earnings/profits | Tax counsel |
| Redemption | Company buys back shares | Can be dividend or sale treatment; complex for foreign owners | Tax counsel |
| Liquidation | Company winds down and distributes assets/cash | Can trigger FIRPTA/USRPHC and other issues | Tax counsel |

Recommended sequence:

1. Repay true loan principal first if loans exist and terms permit.
2. Pay interest only after the CPA confirms withholding and reporting.
3. Pay dividends only after corporate tax reserves and state taxes are funded.
4. Avoid redemptions and liquidations without a written tax review.
5. For foreign owners, prepare Forms 1042-S annually for reportable payments.

## Phase 9 - Scenario-Specific Actions

### Scenario A: U.K. And Belgian Capital Originates From Dubai/UAE

Key point: Dubai banking location does not create U.S.-UAE treaty benefits. The tax question is who owns the money and where that owner is tax resident.

Actions:

1. Obtain source-of-funds documentation from the Dubai accounts.
2. Confirm whether the U.K. partner is a U.K. tax resident and beneficial owner.
3. Confirm whether the Belgian partner is a Belgian tax resident and beneficial owner.
4. Do not rely on portfolio interest if either foreign lender owns 10% or more voting power.
5. Have counsel review whether treaty interest treatment is better than portfolio interest.
6. Document all debt terms before funds are wired.
7. Prepare the bank for enhanced KYC because UAE-origin wires may receive extra review.

Professional review required:

1. Treaty eligibility.
2. Anti-conduit risk.
3. Debt/equity risk.
4. Related-party interest rate.
5. U.S. withholding position.
6. U.K./Belgian home-country reporting.

### Scenario B: U.K. Capital From Dubai, Belgian Capital From U.S. Bank Account

Key point: a Belgian investor using a U.S. bank account is still foreign for U.S. tax purposes unless he is actually a U.S. tax resident.

Actions:

1. Confirm why the Belgian investor has a U.S. bank account and whether it affects U.S. residency analysis.
2. Document the source of the Belgian funds even if the money is already in the United States.
3. Treat the Belgian investment as equity or debt based on legal documents, not bank location.
4. Collect W-8BEN or W-8BEN-E from the Belgian investor or his entity.
5. Review Belgian treaty eligibility.
6. Plan repatriation to Belgium before payments are made.
7. Confirm whether U.S. bank interest, prior U.S. activities, or U.S. days create other tax issues for the Belgian partner.

Professional review required:

1. U.S. residency and NRA status confirmation.
2. Belgian home-country tax reporting.
3. Treaty benefits.
4. Estate tax exposure if direct U.S. shares are held.
5. Withholding on interest or dividends.

## Best Financial Setup, In Plain Terms

The financially best setup is probably not the most aggressive structure. The venture should optimize for after-tax cash that survives review by banks, title companies, the IRS, and foreign tax authorities.

The strongest starting position is:

1. Use the U.S. C corporation blocker for operations.
2. Solve estate tax exposure before foreign partners hold U.S. shares directly.
3. Use debt only where it has real lender economics and proper withholding support.
4. Use treaty benefits only where residency, beneficial ownership, W-8 forms, and limitation-on-benefits rules are documented.
5. Keep clean banking and accounting from the first wire.
6. Treat professional review as part of the setup cost, not optional overhead.

That approach may cost more upfront, but it reduces the chance that later profits are trapped by withholding errors, estate tax exposure, bank delays, title problems, or IRS penalties.
